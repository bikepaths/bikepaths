Long-distance hitching represents one of the purest forms of nomadic travel, stripping transit down to patience, human generosity, and roadside instinct. In the second installment of discussions recorded by writer <a href="https://amzn.to/47qIzzN">Andrew M. Crusoe</a>, lifelong wanderer <a href="https://bikepaths.org/topics/irvthomas.com/">Irv Thomas</a> reflects upon six decades spent traveling across North America and Europe. His journeys demonstrate that traveling the open road requires practical craft, sharp awareness, and an enduring trust in ordinary citizens.

Rather than approaching travel as a paid consumer trip, Thomas treated the roadside as a deep school for personal growth. His reflections, chronicled in his book Derelict Days, reveal how stepping away from social habit builds self-reliance and emotional grit. Examining his practical methods offers valuable lessons for modern wanderers seeking authentic human ties outside mainstream tour packages.

**Practical Roadcraft and Urban Exit Strategies**

Successful hitching depends on understanding traffic flow and road choice. Thomas strongly cautioned against attempting to catch rides from the crowded centers of large cities. Urban traffic moves quickly across complex multi-lane freeways, making it difficult for drivers to pull over and creating hazards for walkers. Instead, experienced road nomads take public transit to the outer edge of a town, positioning themselves on primary outbound roads.

Choosing the proper roadside spot boosts the chance of securing sound transit. Thomas favored two-lane state routes and county roads where car speeds stay moderate and drivers have ample time to watch the wanderer. A good spot features a wide shoulder where drivers can pull over without blocking following cars. Presenting a clean, friendly look while keeping polite eye contact reassures oncoming drivers and builds early trust.

Traveling with a partner gives mutual help and lowers inner worry, especially for new nomads. While securing rides for two people sometimes takes longer, companionship offers shared judgment when weighing open rides. Thomas noted that trusting gut feelings remains the core rule of personal protection on the road. When an approaching car or driver sparks doubt, a traveler stays free by politely turning down the ride.

**Cultural Variations and Transnational Travel Realities**

Roadside customs shift across borders and regional cultures. In his European travels, Thomas discovered that local views toward roadside nomads mirror broad social habits and legal codes. He noted that traveling through Great Britain was very welcoming, praising the kindness and talkative ways of British drivers. By contrast, traveling through continental lands often required adapting to new hand signals and different ideas about transit habits.

In the United States, hitching thrived during the mid-twentieth century before facing broad cultural discouragement. Sensational news stories and growing car dependency cast suspicion upon unmediated roadside rides. Despite this public chill, Thomas continued hitching well into his eighties, showing that individual kindness lives on beneath social fears. Outside online chat boards and rideshare apps, the open road continues to link people across diverse social lines.

Facing unexpected twists forms a natural part of nomadic life. Thomas often recounted funny tales, including unknowingly riding in a stolen car and talking through awkward moments with unusual drivers. Rather than viewing these events as ruin, he welcomed them as rich story material that deepened his grasp of human nature. Meeting uncertain days with humor and calm prevents road weariness and turns delay into lasting lore.

**Roadside Wisdom and the Recovery of Trust**

Beyond daily travel steps, decades on the road taught Thomas that human beings are far kinder than daily news reports suggest. Modern trade life often trains people to view strangers with persistent dread and cold suspicion. Relying on the goodwill of drivers challenges this defensive wall, proving that regular people gladly go out of their way to help a solitary traveler. Each shared ride becomes a quiet lesson in mutual trust and good cheer.

Hitching also strips away the artificial clock that runs modern life. Without a set timetable, nomads learn to let go of the urge to control every hour, taking delays as natural pauses for rest and thought. Waiting patiently beside an open highway teaches calm poise, tuning the traveler to the physical land and the rhythm of passing towns. This unhurried pace allows warm talks to spark between drivers and guests from very different walks of life.

In the end, the roadside philosophy of Irv Thomas champions a life led by wonder rather than fearful habit. Stepping onto the road takes nerve, yet it rewards the open mind with rich self-knowledge and a lasting faith in everyday people. By stepping outside conventional travel habits, nomadic wanderers find that the world remains wide, friendly, and full of grace.
