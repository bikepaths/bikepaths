Eighty years ago, the United States attempted to teach the world a lasting lesson about power. In the autumn of 1945, amidst the ruined brick piles of southern Germany, an American Supreme Court Justice named Robert Jackson took the podium inside a stone courtroom at Nuremberg. Allied armies had broken the German army, and twenty-one Nazi leaders sat in the prisoner dock facing charges of aggressive war and crimes against humanity.

Justice Jackson did not speak as a conqueror taking revenge. Standing before foreign judges, Jackson spoke for the American people and announced a permanent moral covenant. He told the court that if treaty violations and unprovoked invasions were crimes, they remained crimes whether Germany committed them or whether the United States committed them. He pledged that America would never establish a standard of conduct for adversaries that the country was unwilling to have invoked against itself.

**The Architecture of Exceptionalism**

That promise formed the moral foundation of modern American foreign policy. For decades after the trials in Germany, American leaders told the public that the nation stood for the equal rule of law. When foreign dictators invaded neighbors or leveled civilian neighborhoods, Washington pointed to the Nuremberg charters and demanded international accountability.

Yet beneath the public speeches, a different set of practical safeguards took shape. In the halls of the United Nations in New York, the post-war treaties granted five major powers permanent veto power on the Security Council. Whenever an international agency or judicial panel attempted to investigate actions taken by the American armed forces, the representative from Washington raised a hand and vetoed the motion. The courtroom door remained open for small nations, but closed whenever the superpower stood in the hallway.

Reluctance soon hardened into federal legislation. In the summer of 2002, the United States Congress passed the American Service-Members Protection Act. The bill gained rapid bipartisan support and received the president's signature behind closed doors. European diplomats quickly nicknamed the legislation the Hague Invasion Act. The federal statute grants the President of the United States formal authority to employ any means necessary, including military force, to liberate any American personnel or allied official detained by the International Criminal Court in the Netherlands.

**The Concrete Reality of Immunity**

This protective barrier showed its true cost during recent conflicts across the Middle East. When four cruise missiles struck the Shajareh Tayyebeh elementary school in Minab, Iran, one hundred and twenty children died beneath falling concrete. Defense analysts had rushed outdated coordinates into automated targeting software without verifying whether children occupied the building.

Under the Nuremberg principles, a missile strike on a crowded school constitutes an undeniable atrocity. Yet in the real world of modern diplomacy, no international marshal arrives with an arrest warrant. Foreign courts cannot subpoena flight logs, and international human rights bodies cannot freeze the bank accounts of the senior officials who ordered the attack. The protective umbrella of military and economic power shields the chain of command from outside inquiry.

This practical immunity exposes why the language of diplomacy changed over the past twenty years. Politicians and commentators retired the phrase "international law," replacing it with the looser term "rules-based international order." Real law requires equal enforcement across all parties, backed by impartial judges who cannot be dismissed by the defendant. A rules-based order functions as a discretionary club where the most powerful member writes the regulations, decides when exemptions apply, and shields its own personnel from review.

**The Sanitized Screen**

The absence of external legal restraint means that American citizens hold the only remaining gavel. Yet the domestic public remains largely unaware of this responsibility, kept in the dark by four powerful barriers.

The first barrier is the sanitization of military reporting. Major media outlets rarely broadcast the physical aftermath of airstrikes in foreign towns. News reports replace the visceral reality of pulverized classrooms and mourning families with dry, bureaucratic phrases like precision engagements, kinetic strikes, and collateral damage. When violence is reduced to technical jargon, citizens cannot visualize the human cost of government decisions.

**The Insulated Citizenry**

The second barrier is the removal of personal civilian sacrifice. During the world wars of the twentieth century, military campaigns required draft calls, wartime income surcharges, and consumer rationing. Every household felt the friction of international conflict through personal sacrifice.

Today, an all-volunteer military comprised of less than one percent of the population fights distant conflicts funded through an expanding federal debt. Everyday citizens face no draft notice and pay no visible war tax, so foreign military campaigns operate as distant background noise. Civilian life continues uninterrupted while cruise missiles fly across foreign borders on borrowed money.

**The Bipartisan Consensus**

The third barrier is the lack of political choice on election day. Democratic elections offer real debate on domestic questions like taxes, healthcare, and education. When the conversation turns to the use of military power abroad, both major political parties share the same fundamental posture.

Both parties routinely vote to expand the annual defense budget past eight hundred billion dollars. Both parties defend the use of the veto power in the United Nations Security Council to block inquiries into allied conduct. Both parties reject the jurisdiction of the International Criminal Court to investigate their actions abroad. Presidential candidates from both major factions endorse sovereign immunity, so the ballot box rarely provides voters with a way to demand accountability for foreign civilian casualties.

**The Moral Exception Myth**

The fourth barrier is a deeply rooted national mythology of inherent benevolence. From elementary school classrooms to Hollywood movies, many Americans learn that their nation functions as a unique guardian of global liberty.

This cultural belief creates a double standard in the public mind. When foreign adversaries bomb cities, the domestic press describes the violence as deliberate savagery and demands war crimes tribunals. When American munitions level an Iranian schoolhouse or an Afghan hospital, public officials dismiss the tragedy as an unfortunate technological malfunction. The public accepts the explanation because citizens believe their government acts with pure intentions, blind to the reality that a child killed by a guided missile is just as dead regardless of who fired the munition.

**The Sole Juror**

These four barriers have severed the connection between the ballot box and the consequences of foreign policy. The system has walled off its political and military leadership from international justice, leaving the strong free from external restraint.

That diplomatic victory leaves an immense burden upon the American voting public. Foreign judges in Europe hold no power to summon an American president or defense secretary to answer for their conduct. The American voter remains the only judge left with that power on the planet.

When citizens accept sanitized language, ignore foreign civilian casualties, and return politicians to office without demanding accountability for destroyed schools and hospitals, they ratify the doctrine that raw power overrides justice. The covenant announced by Justice Jackson in that stone German courtroom does not depend on foreign treaties or distant tribunals. It depends on whether regular men and women in the voting booth hold their own leaders accountable to the foundational truth that innocent human life carries the same value everywhere.
